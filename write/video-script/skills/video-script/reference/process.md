# Process notes

## Spoken English

A listener hears each line once, in order, and cannot scroll back.

- Contractions, short sentences, one idea per sentence.
- Every sentence has its own nouns and a verb. A pointer ("each one", "it") needs its noun in the same sentence or the one before.
- One name for one thing. Explain a trade term once in one plain sentence, then keep the word.
- No label lines such as "the short answer:". A phrase the listener must stop and unpack fails, even when every word is right.
- A command or setting on screen is a label. Never read it aloud.
- Write for the viewer, not for search. Search phrases belong in the description.

## Flow

About four in ten sentences in a script that flows open on a word a listener hears as a link: "so", "then", "but", "when", or a question asked aloud. Write each chapter as one paragraph first, so each sentence follows the one before. Then cut it into rows.

Open each chapter from where the story is. Ask the chapter's question aloud. Give one to three sentences that say why it comes now. Then answer in a full sentence that uses the question's own words.

After every event in the story, say what it means before anyone acts on it. An event followed straight by an action sounds like a list.

## The checks, in order

Each goes to a fresh reader. A reader that wrote the script cannot hear its gaps.

1. **House checks.** Never-say words, numbers without a source, a missing source cell.
2. **Flow read.** The reader sees the narration with no headings. It outlines each chapter in one sentence and checks the outline is one argument. It marks each sentence as follows, turns, or jumps. Done when there are no jumps.
3. **Fact check.** Line by line against the sources, with a verdict and the deciding words. It tests scope: a line may not claim more than its source measured. Another company's product is checked against its own pages read that day.
4. **Missing-context read.** The reader plays the viewer and reads one line at a time, never ahead. It flags pointers, squeezed phrases, shorthand, sentences nobody would say, jumps, label lines, and things the viewer already knows. It also says what one idea the video teaches and where it would stop watching. Every new reader finds new flags, so the count never reaches zero. Fix what changes the listen, not the count.
5. **Cut.** One reader removes detours and repeats. It keeps the words that tell a listener where they are, every chapter question and every condition.
6. **Second fact check** on every changed row and the rows beside each cut. Fixes change facts.
7. **First-time viewer read.** A reader who knows only the title hears each line once and flags only what loses them. It checks the video answers its title plainly and early enough for an assistant to quote.
8. **Head-to-head.** Readers from more than one model family choose blind between this script and its last version, in both orders, and say where they stop listening. A judge that picks whichever came second both times is a tie.

How to apply findings: fix or keep with a reason. A fix rewrites the paragraph, then re-cuts rows. When a check makes you cut a line that carried the story's stakes, put true stakes back in the same place.

## The rough cut

Voice the exact current script and write down the script's row ids and a fingerprint of each line. A check can then prove the cut matches the script. Transcribe each clip and compare it with its line. Use the voice the video will use, or say it is a stand-in.

Open the review with one line: what to watch, a link, how many minutes it takes. Show the packaging beside the cut, not after the approver reacts.

## Packaging

**Chapters:** timestamps come from the rough cut. Without audio they are estimates. **Thumbnail idea:** one picture and at most four words. **End screen:** the next video's card, or none for the last video.

**Description:** the viewer's situation from the video's story, then "in this video, we" with what it covers in order. Then the article line, the chapters (the close last), "next in this playlist", and the company's one-line about. No word about the voice.

**Tags:** 10 to 16, each backed by a part of the video, under 500 characters counted the way the platform counts them. Commas count. A tag with a space counts two quotation marks. Tags matter little for discovery. Title, thumbnail and description matter more.

## The company's part

The company comes in only where the story needs what it does. Ask: what would the company do for you that you could not do yourself after watching this? If the answer is a feature the video just taught, the part is weak. Never open it with "since this video is from". Use an approved line only if it sounds right spoken.

## Severity of a finding

A finding that would mislead a viewer or break a source's scope blocks the handover. A line that is hard to follow once is fixed or kept with a reason.

## Missing or conflicting sources

No facts file or approved wording: write the brief and chain, mark each claim UNSOURCED, write no number, and ask the approver for sources before step 3. Two sources give different values: cut the value from the script, record both in `checks/`, and ask the approver which wins. No article exists: drop the article line from the close and say so.
